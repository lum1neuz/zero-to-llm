---
paper: TinyStories
gist: Language models with only a few million parameters can write fluent, coherent stories if they're trained on a synthetic dataset of simple stories that uses a small child's vocabulary.
---

## The question {#question ref="§1"}
> Small models trained on web text produce incoherent text; is that because they're small, or because web text is too broad for them?

Models of around 100M parameters trained on general corpora rarely stay coherent for more than a sentence or two. The authors suspected the data asks too much: web text covers every topic, style and fact. They built a dataset that keeps the core of language, grammar and simple reasoning, and drops most of the breadth.

## The dataset {#dataset ref="§2"}
> Short stories generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, using only words a 3- to 4-year-old would typically understand.

Each generation prompt asks for a story containing a few randomly chosen words, such as a verb, a noun and an adjective, and sometimes features like dialogue or a twist ending. The random constraints keep millions of stories from collapsing into a few templates. The result is a large corpus that is simple in vocabulary but varied in content.

## Small models, real coherence {#results ref="§3–4"}
> Models below 10 million parameters, and even models with a single Transformer block, produce grammatical, consistent stories.

The paper trains a range of sizes and depths and finds that grammar comes first, then consistency, then creativity, as models grow. Your 30M model is larger than most of theirs, which is why its stories read well after half an epoch.

## GPT-4 as a grader {#grading ref="§3"}
> The authors ask GPT-4 to grade the stories' grammar, creativity and consistency, like a teacher marking homework.

Perplexity alone doesn't say whether a story makes sense. A model completes the start of a story, and GPT-4 scores the completion. This makes it possible to compare model sizes on qualities that are hard to measure automatically.

## Interpretability {#interpretability ref="§6"}
> Small models trained on this data have attention heads and neurons that are easier to interpret.

Some attention heads clearly attend by distance and others by meaning, for example from a pronoun to the character it refers to. Simple data and small models make these mechanisms easier to see than in large web-trained models.

## How the course uses it {#course}
> 1.4 trains a 30M model on TinyStories, and phase 3 quantizes that model.

A small model that clearly works gives you a baseline perplexity for the quantization lessons, without a large GPU bill. The course tokenizer differs from the paper's, so perplexities aren't directly comparable with the paper's numbers.
